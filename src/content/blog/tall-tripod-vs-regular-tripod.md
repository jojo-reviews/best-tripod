In the tall tripod vs regular tripod choice, a tall tripod wins if you are about 6 feet or taller, shoot on slopes or over fences, or work in wind; a regular tripod wins on weight, folded length and stiffness for everyone else. The number that decides it is the height of the tripod legs alone, with the center column down.

When a tall reader asks us which class to buy, we start with that one figure and our chart of [how tall a tripod should be for your height](/blog/how-tall-should-a-tripod-be-for-my-height/), then weigh what the extra leg length costs.

## Tall tripod vs regular tripod height, legs only

**A regular tripod has legs that extend [50 to 63 inches](https://www.breathingcolor.com/blogs/news/how-to-buy-the-right-tripod), while a tall tripod reaches about 60 inches or more before the center column moves.** When we compare two spec sheets, we ignore the headline "maximum height" first, because it usually includes a fully raised column.

Here is how that plays out with two real tripods we use as reference points, a full-size regular model and a budget tall one:

| Spec | Regular: Manfrotto MT055XPRO3 | Tall: K&F Concept 90 inch Tripod |
|---|---|---|
| Legs only, column down | 55 in (B&H spec sheet) | [63.8 in](https://www.kfconcept.com/KF09.119_90-tall-overhead-aluminum-camera-tripod-monopod) (maker) |
| Maximum, column up | 67 in | 90.6 in |
| Estimated viewfinder, column down | 60 to 63 in | about 64 to 72 in |
| Weight | 5.5 lb, legs only | 4.46 lb with ball head |
| Folded length | 24 in | 21.3 in |
| Leg sections | 3 | 4 |

Our viewfinder estimate adds 5 to 8 inches for a tripod head and a mirrorless or DSLR camera (and allows nothing at the low end for the K&F, in case its figure already counts the ball head). A 6-foot photographer's eye level sits at about 67 to 68 inches, so the regular tripod leaves that person 5 to 7 inches short, and the only way up is the column. In our view, that gap is the whole tall tripod vs regular tripod question in one line.

## When a tall tripod beats a regular tripod

**A tall tripod beats a regular tripod in four situations: a photographer of 6 feet or more, sloping ground, a view blocked by a fence or crowd, and wind.**

The clearest case we found is a DPReview member who traded a Gitzo GT5531S that already reached his eye level for a taller version. His three reasons:

- **Fences and hedges.** Extra leg length lifts the lens over walls, hedges and crowds.
- **Steep slopes.** The downhill leg can extend much further, so the camera stays high instead of dropping toward the hillside.
- **High winds.** Long legs can be spread wider for a lower, steadier stance while still giving a usable working height.

We add a fourth reason to his list: your own height. Stooping over a short tripod is a recurring complaint in tall-photographer threads, and cranking the column up to avoid it brings its own problem: one Photography Stack Exchange answer warns that "center post can cause vibration and cost rigidity (especially in a windy environment)". Our fixes for [tripod shake in wind](/blog/how-to-stop-tripod-shake-in-wind/) start with exactly that column.

## What a tall tripod costs in weight, folded length and stiffness

**Within one tripod family, the tall version is heavier and slightly less stiff at the same height: The Center Column found a long model should score [11% lower](https://thecentercolumn.com/explorations/tripod-stiffness-vs-height/) than its standard sibling.**

- **Weight.** "The big problem is weight," one member writes in that DPReview thread. Carbon fiber legs are the usual answer if you hike.
- **Folded length.** A member in the same thread calls his tall tripod, at about 70 cm folded, "basically to long to fly with". We would measure the suitcase or backpack before the tripod.
- **Stiffness at full extension.** Budget tall tripods flip the weight trade. The K&F is lighter and shorter folded than the Manfrotto, but it gets its height from four sections that taper to 16 mm tubes. We would set its legs to eye level and keep the thinnest section in reserve; one leg also detaches as a monopod, which is handy when a full tripod will not fit.
- **Low angles.** The Manfrotto drops to 3.5 inches, low enough for macro photography; the K&F stops at 26.2 inches unless you reverse its column.

> **From our notes:** The Manfrotto MT055XPRO3's 55-inch legs-only figure is what reframed this comparison for us. Its 67-inch maximum looks tall enough for almost anyone, but B&H's spec sheet shows a full 12 inches of that comes from the column. The K&F's legs alone stand 63.8 inches. What we took away: the two classes differ less in maximum height than in where the height comes from, the legs or the column.

## Which tripod height should you get, tall or regular?

**Get a tall tripod if your height minus 10 to 12 inches is more than the legs-only height of the regular tripod you are looking at.** Teds Cameras draws the class line in metric: regular tripods run 130 to 160 cm, tall ones 180 cm and up.

- The rule says regular and you shoot on mostly flat ground: my pick is a regular tripod, lighter, stiffer and cheaper.
- The rule says tall, or you shoot on slopes, over fences or into crowds: a tall tripod, used short of full extension.
- Frequent flights: whichever folds to fit your carry-on.
