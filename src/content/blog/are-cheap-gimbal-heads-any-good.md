I have been shooting since 2017, and wildlife is one of the genres I keep coming back to. For wildlife I use a 200-600mm lens, and on a long sit that lens is exactly the kind of weight a gimbal head is built for. So when asked whether a cheap one is worth it, I start from that zoom, the same one my [gimbal head for a 150-600mm lens](/blog/best-gimbal-head-for-150-600mm-lens/) guide is built around.

Are cheap gimbal heads any good? Yes: a cheap gimbal head holds a 150-600mm or 200-500mm zoom well for occasional use, as long as you accept stiffer, less refined motion. It is also the lowest-risk way to learn whether you will use a gimbal at all; the two budget heads worth a look are the aluminum Oben GH-30 and the carbon Sirui PH-20. I would step up to a premium tripod head only for a 500mm or 600mm f/4 prime used for long sessions, or for daily tracking of birds in flight.

## What a Cheap Gimbal Head Does Well

**A cheap gimbal head does the core job: it holds a telephoto lens at its balance point so the rig feels weightless and stays where you leave it. What you lose is smoothness, precision and some trust in the locks.**

The problem I am solving is simple: a big zoom gets heavy fast. A gimbal head is a tripod head that clamps the lens foot, not the camera, and pivots the telephoto lens around its center of gravity. It is not a motorized video gimbal like the DJI RS 3 Pro I use for video.

The forum owners split in a useful way. On DPReview, one owner wrote, "I would steer well clear of cheap gimbals. You are looking for solid construction and very smooth bearings." Another in [the same 180-600 thread](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/threads/gimbals-for-180-600-and-wildlife.4768954/) answered, "These inexpensive gimbals are perfectly adequate for determining whether or not he'll use one." I think both are right, for different shooters.

## What a Cheap Gimbal Head Gives Up

**A budget gimbal saves money in the parts that move: bushings instead of bearings, thick grease instead of tuned friction, and simpler locks and clamps.**

This is the part I look at hardest, since the money is saved here. Digital Camera World notes that cheaper gimbals can add slack and wobble; owner threads give the details.

### Bushings, grease and stiffness

On generic Beike-type heads, owners describe nylon or plastic bushings where premium heads use bearings. A Nikonites owner put it plainly: "i think the main area of cheapness is nylon bushes instead of bearings." A DPReview owner with a Beike found the bush had "a mixture of play and stiffness" and fitted ball races on a lathe. Another, with a budget Neewer head, removed the factory grease, regreased it with white lithium grease and said it then worked very well.

The stiffness is not all bad. A Talk Photography owner found a Beike BK-45 fine for static birds and video, but too stiff for birds in flight. Another warns that some plastic bushings swell when lubricated, so check the grease first.

### Lock shift and clamp quality

Lock shift means the framing moves when you tighten a knob. One Talk Photography owner returned a BK-45 because the locks moved the head considerably and the Arca-Swiss clamp felt crude. My whole support kit runs on Arca-Swiss plates, so a crude clamp would bother me more than stiff motion. Mid-priced heads are not immune: Shutter Muse saw a diagonal shift when tightening the Sirui PH-20 tilt knob. Lock shift matters for static compositions and panoramas, and little when you track with the knobs loose.

### Weight and bulk

Budget aluminum gimbal heads are heavy. The Oben GH-30 weighs 3.15 lb per its manual, the same figure Backcountry Journeys lists for a Wimberley WH-200. A DPReview owner noted that gimbals are "relatively large and heavy to transport, even those that break down into three separate parts." If weight is your limit, look at side mount designs: Shutter Muse measured the Sirui PH-20 23% lighter in side mount mode.

## Rated Load vs Your Real Lens

**A cheap gimbal head's load rating says what it can hold, not how smoothly it moves at that weight. Most budget heads are rated far above a zoom rig, so the rating is rarely the problem.**

Oben rates the GH-30 for 44 lb (20 kg). A Nikonites owner put a Nikon 200-500mm plus a D610 at about 7 pounds, and a Backcountry Gallery owner gave 11.25 lb for a 600mm f/4G VR. Both sit well under the load rating. Yet Shutter Muse warns not to take the GH-30 rating at face value: it loses smoothness with very heavy setups, and he calls it OK with lighter lenses such as a 70-200mm, 100-400mm, 200-600mm or Nikon Z 400mm f/4.5. A 200-600mm like mine sits in that comfortable group.

My rule: size a gimbal head for smooth motion with your heaviest lens, not for the number on the box, and never load it beyond the rating.

A zoom lens adds a catch. A DPReview owner found his 200-500mm tipped slightly at the long end because the barrel extends. That happens on any gimbal, so balance at the focal length you use most.

## Two Budget Gimbal Heads the Ranking Guides Agree On

**These two lower-cost gimbal heads are each recommended by at least two of the pages that rank for this question.**

I chose from the products the top results recommend, then checked specs against the manual or B&H and read owner threads.

### Oben GH-30 Gimbal Head: Best budget full gimbal

The [Oben GH-30 Gimbal Head](amzn:Oben+GH-30+Gimbal+Head) is an aluminum cradle gimbal head from Oben, and Shutter Muse, Backcountry Journeys and B&H eXplora all name it as the affordable full gimbal. What matters to me here is real balance: its curved arm carries a plate platform you slide up and down, so you can line the lens center up with the tilt axis. Oben's manual lists 44 lb (20 kg), 3.15 lb, a 360° panning base, a 3/8" socket and an Arca-type plate with two 1/4" screws plus 3/8" bushings. Owners are mixed but mostly content. A Backcountry Gallery owner says it balances his Nikon 600mm TC rig fine, though the movement is "a bit rough." A Fred Miranda owner found the head stiff and then leaking grease after a couple of years; Oben replaced it with a new, smoother unit. Shutter Muse calls it a good option for the odd weekend excursion. I'd pick this if you want a full cradle for little money and don't mind the weight.

### Sirui PH-20 Gimbal Head: Best step-up carbon gimbal

The [Sirui PH-20 Gimbal Head](amzn:SIRUI+PH-20+Gimbal+Head) is a carbon fiber gimbal head from Sirui that buys smoother motion and a lighter build than the cheapest tier. B&H lists 44.1 lb (20 kg), 2.4 lb and a 120 mm Arca-type plate. Shutter Muse measured 1,163 g with the cradle and 899 g after swapping in the included side mount clamp. He found the pan and tilt silky smooth, but also a 2-3° scale misalignment and a framing shift when tightening the tilt knob. Owners echo both sides. A Backcountry Gallery owner used it for hours in Costa Rica with a 180-600mm, while a DPReview buyer found the pan lock moved the framing. One Shutter Muse reader sent his back twice because the knobs would not stop a 500mm f/4 rig from moving when carried. I'd pick this if you carry the head often and the side mount option saves you weight.

> **From our notes:** Putting the Oben manual beside the Sirui spec sheet and three forum threads about generic gimbals, I found a pattern that surprised me. The load ratings barely differed: 44 lb for the Oben and the Sirui. What differed was everything the spec sheet leaves out. Owners of the cheapest heads wrote about plastic bushings, sticky grease and locks that nudge the framing. Shutter Muse and owners wrote about the PH-20's smooth bearings but also its tilt-lock shift. So I stopped comparing ratings and started asking one question: how will the head be used?

**Which one I'd pick for you**

- A 150-600mm zoom on weekend trips: the Oben GH-30.
- Lighter kit, frequent use or hiking: the Sirui PH-20, with its side mount clamp.
- Just testing whether a gimbal suits you: a generic Beike-type head, regreased.
- A 500mm or 600mm f/4 prime every week: skip both and buy a Wimberley-class head.

## Cheap Gimbal Head vs Wimberley: When to Pay More

**Pay for a premium gimbal head when the lens is a big prime, the sessions are long and tracking smoothness decides the shot. For a zoom and occasional outings, a budget gimbal is enough.**

The table below compares the three tiers on the points owners and guides raise most.

| Tier | Example | Rated load | Motion, per owners and reviews | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Generic cheap gimbal | Beike BK-45 type | Varies by listing | Stiff, plastic bushings; regreasing helps | Hides, static subjects, trying a gimbal |
| Budget brand | Oben GH-30 | 44 lb (20 kg) | Smooth enough, can feel rough | 150-600mm zooms, weekend use |
| Mid-priced carbon | Sirui PH-20 | 44.1 lb (20 kg) | Silky smooth, lock shift reported | Lighter kit, frequent use |
| Mid-priced aluminum | Benro GH2 | 50 lb, per Backcountry Journeys | Solid and fluid, per Backcountry Journeys | A step up without premium cost |
| Premium | Wimberley WH-200 | Over 100 lb, per B&H | The benchmark for smoothness | 500-600mm f/4 primes, daily use |

A budget gimbal head fits if you shoot a 150-600mm, 180-600mm or 200-500mm zoom a few times a year, mostly wait on one subject at a nest or hide, or want to try a gimbal before spending more. Premium does not always mean more load: Backcountry Journeys lists the Gitzo Gimbal Fluid Head at 17.6 lb, built for fluid video and stills work rather than the heaviest primes.

Choose a premium head if you track birds in flight from a tripod every week, or if you shoot a 500mm or 600mm f/4 prime for hours at a time. Talk Photography's owners put it simply: cheap heads "do the job but without any finesse."

## Problems Owners Run Into With Budget Gimbals, and the Fix

**The common problems with a cheap gimbal head are that it goes unused, it is too heavy to carry, or the legs are too light. Each has a cheap fix.**

1. **The gimbal stays home.** A DPReview owner with a 200-500mm wrote, "I have used it less than I anticipated," thanks to VR and IBIS. The fix is a cheap head first, or a monopod. My [monopods with feet for wildlife](/blog/best-monopod-with-feet-for-wildlife-photography/) guide suits a 180-600mm on the move.
2. **It is too heavy to hike with.** Carry the gimbal only when you plan a long sit, and use a ball head the rest of the time; ball heads are what my own kit is built around. Gimbal adapters that mount on a ball head need a strong one, and one DPReview owner found a Wimberley Sidekick unusable on his Sirui K-30x ball head. My [ball head tripod guide](/blog/ball-head-tripod/) covers sizing a ball head by lens weight.
3. **The legs shake.** Digital Camera World compares a gimbal on a cheap travel tripod to a fine lock on a flimsy door. Keep the center column down and choose legs rated well above the rig; my guide to the [tripod for wildlife photography](/blog/tripod-for-wildlife-photography/) explains which legs hold a long lens steady.
4. **The plate slides or the lens won't balance.** Use a long Arca-Swiss plate on the lens foot, and check that your plate fits the clamp. My explainer on [Arca-Swiss plates and clamps](/blog/what-is-an-arca-swiss-plate-and-do-i-need-one/) shows why some plates fit loosely.

## Cheap Gimbal Head Verdict for a 150-600mm Zoom

**For a heron at a nest and a 150-600mm zoom, a cheap gimbal head is good enough. I would start with the Oben GH-30, or the Sirui PH-20 if lighter weight and smoother motion matter.**

With a stiff generic head, I would regrease it first, then balance at the focal length I use most. Lock the knobs only when you leave the rig. If the gimbal becomes a weekly habit with a big prime, that is the time to buy a Wimberley-class head. The next question is usually the legs: start with my [tripod for wildlife photography](/blog/tripod-for-wildlife-photography/) guide, and browse more [tripod heads](/category/tripod-heads/) when you are ready.
