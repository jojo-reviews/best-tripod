In the travel tripod vs full size tripod for landscape photography debate, the travel tripod is enough for most shoots: with a wide or standard zoom, the center column down and a calm-to-breezy day, a good carbon travel tripod gives the same sharp frame as a big one. The full-size tripod earns its extra weight in four places: strong wind, moving water, steep slopes and lenses of 200mm and longer. If those describe your usual shoots, carry the big one; if you fly or hike long miles, the travel tripod is the one that actually comes along.

Picture the moment most readers describe. You're on a ridge before sunrise, the ND filter is on, and the 30-second exposure comes back soft because the little tripod hummed in the wind. We built this guide around that shot.

## Travel tripod vs full size tripod for landscape photography – the short answer

**A travel tripod trades stiffness, payload and height for a fold of about 40 cm; a full-size tripod trades packed size for steadiness in bad conditions.** Every top result we read agrees on the basics: travel models are lighter and shorter when folded, more leg sections mean more locks and more flex, and a raised center column costs stability. Landscape photographer Jeff Schultz goes further and tells readers to "throw the compact idea out the window" ([his guide](https://www.schultzphoto.com/2020/08/27/how-to-choose-a-tripod-for-landscape-wildlife-photographer/)), while a [Talk Photography owner](https://www.talkphotography.co.uk/threads/travel-tripod-v-regular-tripod.613452/) found his travel tripod held up in Iceland's wind with the column down.

Both are right, for different shooters. The rest of this guide shows where the line sits.

## Travel tripod vs full-size tripod from one maker – the numbers

**A travel tripod vs full-size tripod comparison is cleanest within one maker's lineup, built from the same carbon tubing.** Gitzo sells the Traveler Series 1 (GT1545T) as its reverse-folding travel model and the Mountaineer Series 2 (GT2542) as its full-size all-rounder. Both have four sections and a twist lock on each joint.

This table compares the maker's published figures for the two sets of legs, without heads:

| Spec (legs only, maker figures) | Traveler Series 1 GT1545T | Mountaineer Series 2 GT2542 |
|---|---|---|
| Weight | [1.055 kg (2.33 lb)](https://www.gitzo.com/us-en/tripod-traveler-series-1-4-sections-gt1545tus/) | [1.68 kg (3.7 lb)](https://www.gitzo.com/us-en/tripod-mountaineer-series-2-4-sections-gt2542/) |
| Folded length | 42.5 cm (16.73 in) | 56 cm (22.05 in) |
| Max height, column down | 130 cm (51.18 in) | 137.5 cm (54.13 in) |
| Max height, column up | 153 cm | 165.5 cm |
| Min height | 22 cm | 15 cm |
| Top leg tube | 25.3 mm | 29 mm |
| Leg angles | 2 (25°, 70°) | 3 (23°, 55°, 83°) |
| Safety payload | 10 kg | 22 kg |
| Recommended lenses | 135mm (200mm max) | 200mm (up to 300mm) |

So the full-size tripod costs you 625 g and 13.5 cm of packed length. In return you get more than twice the payload, a thicker top tube, 7.5 cm more height before the column moves and a third leg angle for low foregrounds.

The lens line is the most useful row. Gitzo says the Traveler is [recommended for use with 135mm lenses (200mm max.)](https://www.gitzo.com/us-en/tripod-kit-traveler-series-1-4-sections-gk1545t-82tqdus/), while the Mountaineer [reliably supports DSLRs with 200mm lenses (up to 300mm)](https://www.gitzo.com/us-en/tripod-mountaineer-series-2-4-sections-gt2542/). If your landscape kit stops at a 70-200mm, you sit right on the border.

> **From our notes:** When we lined up Gitzo's Traveler and Mountaineer spec pages, we expected the weight gap to be the headline. It isn't: 625 g is roughly one standard zoom. The rows that changed our view were further down. The full-size legs carry more than twice the rated payload, add a third leg angle, and Gitzo itself moves the recommended lens from 135mm to 200mm. That told us the real question isn't "how heavy," it's "which lens and which weather."

## Where a travel tripod falls short and a full-size tripod wins

**A full-size tripod pulls ahead in wind, in water, with long lenses and on slopes, where a travel tripod runs out of stiffness or height first.** Owners describe the same few situations again and again.

### Wind and long exposures

The most common complaint in DPReview threads: a small travel tripod feels too wobbly once ND filters, astro or a 100-400mm join the kit. One owner put the rule simply: "The center column make the Travel Tripod less stable so you should not extend it" ([thread](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/threads/travel-tripod-stability-comparison.4834397/)). Another long-exposure shooter wrote: "For long time exposures and astro I would not buy a tripod with more than 3 leg locks" ([thread](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/threads/tripod-for-travel-hiking-long-exposure-stability-vs-portability.4575894/page-2)).

A landscape owner of both classes on [Fred Miranda](https://www.fredmiranda.com/forum/topic/1611148/) found his Series 1 Traveler fine in wind with wide lenses. The Series 2, he wrote, "REALLY shines" in moving water, heavy wind or with a telephoto in wind. We'd use the National Weather Service's Beaufort scale as a practical line: by force 5, a [fresh breeze of 19-24 mph](https://www.weather.gov/mfl/beaufort), small trees in leaf begin to sway, and that's where we'd want the full-size legs for anything longer than a standard zoom.

Hanging your bag to fight vibration helps less than you'd think. The Center Column hung weight from tripods [2.5 lbs at a time up to 20 lbs](https://thecentercolumn.com/2018/02/18/stiffness-and-damping-vs/) and found no change in stiffness and very little in damping. It does lower the center of gravity, so the tripod is less likely to blow over. Our [fixes for tripod shake in wind](/blog/how-to-stop-tripod-shake-in-wind/) cover what does work.

### Height on slopes and the center column

**A full-size tripod is taller with the column down, which matters most on uneven terrain; on level ground a travel tripod is often tall enough.** A recurring point in the DPReview hiking thread is that "a travel pod will be shorter requiring you to bend over to look through the VF," and the same poster added: "I avoid using the center post when possible but in a travel pod thats even more important" ([thread](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/threads/lightest-tripod-for-hiking.4678070/)).

Andrea Livieri, who moved from a Benro travel tripod to a tall full-size model, describes the slope problem in [his review](https://www.andrealivieriphoto.com/blog/flm-cp30-l4-ii-tripod-review). Stretch one or two legs downhill and even a short photographer finds the tripod at navel height. He also swaps rubber feet for spikes on soft ground. The video below shows his side-by-side check of a travel tripod with a center column against taller legs without one. Before you buy, add your head's height to the column-down figure and compare it with your eye level; our guide on [how tall a tripod should be for your height](/blog/how-tall-should-a-tripod-be-for-my-height/) does the math, and the [center column up or down for landscapes](/blog/tripod-center-column-up-or-down-for-landscapes/) guide covers when raising it is acceptable.

### Small heads without friction control

Travel kits pair slim legs with slim heads. One hiker noted of a compact integrated head: "there is no friction control of the ball (it is pretty much on or off with a leaver)" ([thread](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/threads/lightest-tripod-for-hiking.4678070/)). Gitzo states that its Traveler kit head "does not feature a friction control knob." With a light wide-angle that rarely matters; with a heavy lens it makes fine framing slower.

## When a travel tripod is all you need (and a full-size tripod stays home)

**If your landscape kit is a mirrorless or DSLR body with lenses up to about 135mm, a carbon travel tripod covers most shoots.** That fits city trips, flights with carry-on only and long day hikes, where Gitzo's 42.5 cm fold and roughly 1 kg of legs decide whether the tripod comes along at all.

Make it work like a bigger tripod:

1. Extend the thick upper sections first and leave the thinnest bottom section in if you don't need the height.
2. Keep the center column down; raise it last and only a few centimeters.
3. Fire with a 2-second timer or remote, and turn lens stabilization off when the camera is locked down.
4. Lower the whole tripod when the wind picks up; a shorter tripod is a stiffer tripod.

On section count, we compared [4-section vs 5-section legs](/blog/4-section-vs-5-section-tripod/) separately: for landscapes we'd stop at four.

## Travel or full-size tripod – how to choose

**The travel or full-size tripod choice comes down to your longest lens and your worst weather, not the spec sheet's weight line.** We'd use this checklist:

- **Longest lens 135mm or shorter, mostly calm or breezy days, flights or long hikes:** a carbon travel tripod.
- **Lenses of 200mm and longer, coast and river work, strong wind, steep ground, focus stacking:** a full-size tripod with a third leg angle and a top tube near 29 mm.
- **Both kinds of trips:** many owners keep one of each, the travel tripod for planes and trails and the full-size one for drive-up spots.
- **Either way:** an Arca-Swiss plate on the camera so it fits both heads, and our [tripod buying checklist](/tripod-buying-checklist/) before you order.

So for that shooter on the windy ridge: if the lens was a 16-35mm, lower the travel tripod, drop the column and wait for a lull. If it was a 100-400mm, that shot needed the full-size legs.
