  - q: Do you need a special tripod for astrophotography?

      No. Any tripod that stays still for 15 to 30 seconds with the column down

    a: >-
      Add up camera, lens, head and tracker, then multiply by three. A camera,

      For a camera-only kit, yes, if you keep the center column down and stay

      Carbon fiber is lighter for its stiffness and less cold to the touch on

      the Benro Tortoise 24C removes that weak point, at the cost of a fixed
      maximum height.

The best tripod for astrophotography is one rated for about three times the weight of your camera, lens and star tracker, used with the center column down. My best overall pick is the [Benro Tortoise 24C with GX30 Ball Head](amzn:Benro+Tortoise+Columnless+Carbon+Fiber+Tripod+GX30+TTOR24CGX30), a columnless carbon tripod rated for 14 kg at 1.51 kg. If you only shoot a camera and a wide lens, a lighter travel tripod works too, as long as you follow the [center column down rule for long exposures](/blog/tripod-center-column-up-or-down-for-landscapes/).

Picture the scene: you are at a dark site at 1 a.m., the Milky Way core is up, and a 20-second frame shows stars stretched into short dashes. The tracker is fine. The tripod moved. Under a night sky, any vibration shows up for the whole exposure.

## Best Tripod for Astrophotography – The Short Answer

**Match the tripod's rated load to your full night rig, then judge it by its height with the column down.** Here is how I sort the five picks:

- **Best overall for astro:** Benro Tortoise 24C with GX30 Ball Head. Columnless, 14 kg rating, 129.5 cm.
- **Best for flying to dark skies:** Peak Design Travel Tripod (Carbon Fiber). 39.4 cm folded, 1.27 kg.
- **Best for heavy tracker rigs:** Benro Mach3 TMA37C. 16 kg rating, 161.5 cm, legs only.
- **Best for backyard and car-side sessions:** Manfrotto 055 Aluminum 3-Section Tripod Kit with Ball Head. 151.5 cm with the column down.
- **Best budget pick for camera-only nightscapes:** Manfrotto Befree Advanced Aluminum. 128 cm with the column down, ball head included.

> **From our notes:** The 3.6 kg tracker rig is what reshuffled this list. My first draft ranked the tripods by weight carried to the dark site. Then I added up a full-frame body, a 14mm f/1.8 and a Star Adventurer, multiplied by three, and got 10.8 kg. Two of my lightest favorites fell below that line. So I re-sorted by rated load per kilogram you carry, and the columnless Tortoise moved to the top. The weight-first list is still useful, but only for camera-only Milky Way trips.

## The 5 Best Tripods for Astrophotography

**Each astrophotography tripod below solves a different night problem, so start with what sits on top of it: a camera alone, or a camera on a tracker.** The What we like and What we don't like lists sit beside each review.

### Benro Tortoise 24C with GX30 Ball Head: Best overall for astro

I picked the [Benro Tortoise 24C with GX30 Ball Head](amzn:Benro+Tortoise+Columnless+Carbon+Fiber+Tripod+GX30+TTOR24CGX30) as my best overall because it fixes the weakest part of most tripods at night: it has no center column at all. The head sits straight on the leg casting. Benro rates it for [14 kg (30.86 lb)](https://benrousa.com/tortoise-columnless-tripod-2-carbon-fiber-ttor24c-gx30-head-4-sections/) at 1.51 kg with the head. That is 9.3 kg of rated load per kilogram carried, the best ratio in my list.

That rating clears the 10.8 kg target for a camera, a 14mm lens and a small tracker. The GX30 head takes Arca-Swiss style plates, and Benro packs spiked feet for grass or sand.

The owner I read on DPReview reports no camera creep when tightening and calls it light enough for travel. The catch is height. It tops out at 129.5 cm, so a tall shooter will stoop. A forum reply also notes that columnless designs need longer legs, so it folds to 54 cm. I would skip it if you are tall and hate bending to the viewfinder.

### Peak Design Travel Tripod (Carbon Fiber): Best for flying to dark skies

My pick when the dark site is a flight away is the [Peak Design Travel Tripod (Carbon Fiber)](amzn:Peak+Design+Travel+Tripod+carbon+fiber). It folds to 39.4 cm and weighs 1.27 kg. It still gives 130.2 cm of working height with the column down, so you lose almost nothing against the Tortoise.

What I like for astro is the low reach: the column flips for shots down to 14 cm, handy for a foreground rock under the Milky Way. The ball head is included.

Where I'd hold back is the tracker. Its 9.1 kg rating is fine for a camera and a wide prime, about 1.9 kg, but below the three-times margin for a full tracker rig. One owner in a Milky Way photography group called their Peak Design flimsy for night work. Owner reviews elsewhere praise how well it holds up over years and how much weight it saves. ## How Much Load an Astrophotography Tripod Needs With a Star Tracker

**Add up the load on top of the legs, tracker included, then multiply by three.** A full-frame camera, a 14mm lens and a Star Adventurer tracker come to [about 7.9 lbs (3.6 kg)](https://skiesandscopes.com/camera-tripod/), and one night photographer writing for Fstoppers says most pros he speaks to exceed a tripod and head's load capacity by [about three times](https://fstoppers.com/astrophotography/4-tripods-give-best-bang-your-buck-710844). That puts the target near 10.8 kg.

Here is how my five picks line up against two common rigs:

- **Camera and wide lens only (about 1.9 kg, target about 5.7 kg):** all five clear it.
- **Camera, lens and Star Adventurer (3.6 kg, target 10.8 kg):** the Tortoise (14 kg), Mach3 (16 kg) and 055 kit (15 kg) clear it. The Peak Design (9.1 kg) and Befree (9 kg) fall short, though both still beat the 8 kg floor Skies & Scopes suggests.

The rated load per kilogram you carry tells you which tripod gives the most stability for the walk: Tortoise 9.3, Mach3 8.6, Peak Design 7.2, Befree 5.7 and the 055 kit 5.0. Load ratings are maximums, not working loads, so the margin matters more than the headline figure.

Material follows the same logic. Skies & Scopes found the tripods used by Milky Way Photographer of the Year winners were [100% carbon fibre](https://skiesandscopes.com/camera-tripod/) in 2026, with no single model dominating. Carbon gives more stiffness per gram and stays less cold to the touch on a winter night. A full telescope on an equatorial mount is a different class; my guide to the [best tripod for telescope mounts](/blog/best-tripod-for-telescope/) covers that.

**The number that matters at night is the height with the center column down, because that is how you should shoot.** Headline heights include a raised column, and a raised column is where the shake starts.

With the column down, my picks give:

- Peak Design Travel Tripod (Carbon): 130.2 cm

- Manfrotto Befree Advanced: 128 cm

A tripod 10-20 cm under eye level is easier to live with at night than in daytime. Tall shooters should still favor the 055 kit or the Mach3. ## How to Set Up a Tripod for Sharp Stars

**Spread the legs wide, keep the column down and fire the shutter without touching the camera.** These steps work with any of the five tripods:

1. Extend the thickest leg sections first and leave the thinnest ones collapsed.
2. Keep the center column fully down, or use a columnless tripod.
3. Spread the legs to a wide angle and push spiked or rubber feet firmly into the ground.
4. Hang your bag from the hook so it just touches the ground, adding mass without swinging.
5. Block the wind during each exposure: stand upwind of the tripod, or move behind a rock or your car.
6. Use a 2-second self-timer or a remote release, then wait for the head to settle.
7. Check the first frame at 100% on the screen before you shoot the sequence, and wipe dew off the lens between runs.

## Astrophotography Tripod Problems Owners Run Into, and the Fix

**Most night shake comes from five repeat problems, and each has a simple fix.**

**A travel tripod that feels too wobbly.** Photographers who add astro to a small travel tripod hit this, a recurring theme in DPReview threads. One wrote: "i'm afraid the travel one is too small and wobbly for logn exposure with nd filters or astro or when using the 100-400". The fix is load headroom and thicker tubes: the Tortoise or Mach3, or the Peak Design used camera-only with short legs.

**A raised center column that rings.** A reply in r/AskPhotography put it plainly: "The extended center column just serves to amplify and prolong the vibrations you introduced by tripping the shutter." Keep it down, or remove the problem with the columnless Tortoise.

**Wind at an open dark site.** Photo Stack Exchange answers note: "Wind will vibrate across the legs and transmit up to the camera." Lower the tripod, shield it and hang weight. Benro's spiked feet on the Tortoise and Mach3 help on soft ground.

**Leg locks you can't work in gloves.** Owners split on this. One r/photography poster wrote: "I prefer twist because they are easier to deal with with gloves and mittens on, and they're smaller and lighter." Others prefer levers. If you shoot in winter, my rule is a twist lock collar (Tortoise, Mach3) for mittens and levers (Befree, 055 kit, Peak Design cams) for thin gloves.

**No good way to add weight.** Another Photo Stack Exchange answer says: "Some tripods come with a hook that you can hang your bag on to use for your extra mass, which helps a bit." Check the hook before you buy, and let the bag touch the ground so it can't swing.
