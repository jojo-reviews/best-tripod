You reach the overlook twenty minutes before sunrise with seven pounds of camera gear already on your back, and the tripod strapped to the pack suddenly feels like the heaviest thing you own. Then the wind picks up on the ridge, you raise the center column to clear the rocks, and the long exposure comes back soft. The best hiking tripod is the one that solves both halves of that morning: light enough that you actually carry it, and stiff enough at the height you shoot. We built this guide around pack weight, wind and trail ground rather than airports and city breaks, and we matched four tripods to the hikers they suit. We did not use these tripods ourselves; every figure below comes from maker spec pages, retailer listings or owner reports, and we say which.

## Best hiking tripod picks by pack weight

**For most hikers, the best hiking tripod is a full-size carbon travel tripod around 2.3 to 2.8 lb that stands about 51 in with the center column down; a mini tripod only makes sense if you never need standing height.** Here is the quick answer by the kind of hiker you are.

These are our picks, from heaviest to lightest support:

- **Best overall:** the [Peak Design Travel Tripod (Carbon Fiber)](amzn:Peak+Design+Travel+Tripod+Carbon+Fiber), 2.8 lb with its head built in, 15.5 in folded and rated to 20 lb.
- **Lightest full-size legs:** the [Gitzo Traveler GT1545T](amzn:Gitzo+GT1545T+Series+1+Carbon+4+Sections+Traveler+Tripod) at about 2.3 lb, legs only, so you add your own head.
- **Wrap-around mini:** the [JOBY GorillaPod 5K Kit](amzn:Joby+GorillaPod+5K+Kit) at 1.6 lb, for rocks, rails and branches.
- **Pocket backup:** the [Manfrotto PIXI EVO](amzn:Manfrotto+MTPIXIEVO-BK+PIXI+EVO+2-Section+Mini+Tripod) at 0.57 lb, a tabletop tripod for rest stops.

## How we chose these hiking tripods

**We kept only tripods that at least two of the top-ranking hiking and backpacking guides recommend, then checked each one against maker pages, retailer spec tables and owner reviews.** We read Pack Hacker, We Seek Travel, Backwoods Pursuit, J.M. Peltier and Matt Purciel; retailer list pages such as Best Buy's did not count as a recommendation. Plenty of other names turned up only once, so they are not reviewed here.

For each tripod we then checked the trail numbers that matter:

1. Weight with a head, not just the legs.
2. Folded length for a pack strap or side pocket.
3. Working height with the center column down.
4. Rated load against a real kit, such as a mirrorless camera with 70-200mm lens.
5. Leg sections, leg-lock type and how the head adjusts.
6. What owners praise and complain about after real hikes.

> **From our notes:** When we lined up the spec sheets for these four tripods, the weight column turned out to be the trap. The Gitzo's 2.3 lb looks like the lightest full-size option, but that figure is legs and a short column only, while the carbon Peak Design's 2.8 lb already includes its ball head. Add even a small head to the Traveler and the gap nearly closes. The two full-size picks, by contrast, have almost the same column-down height, 51.2 and 51.25 in. That's why we compare weight with a head and height without the column, not the headline numbers.

## Best tripods for hiking and backpacking, reviewed

**Each hiking tripod below fits a different pack and lens, so start with the heaviest lens you carry up the trail.** The What we like and What we don't like lists sit beside each review; the text adds the trail context owners give.

### Peak Design Travel Tripod (Carbon Fiber): best overall for hiking

Pack Hacker and We Seek Travel both recommend this carbon model, and the numbers explain why. [The maker's spec page](https://www.peakdesign.com/products/travel-tripod) says it packs to the diameter of a standard water bottle; B&H lists 15.5 in folded and 2.8 lb, with a 20 lb (9.1 kg) load rating. It stands 51.25 in with the column down and 60 in with it up, and drops to 5.5 in for low foreground shots. Five carbon sections release with aluminum cam levers, and the plate is Arca-type compatible.

Owners on the maker's site and at B&H keep saying the same thing: it's small enough that they now bring a tripod on hikes where they used to leave one at home. One B&H owner put it as "something I don't have to ask myself if I should bring it or not because of it's size and weight!"

The weak points are the head and the latches. The built-in ball head has one adjustment ring and no separate pan, and a B&H review labeled as a promotional "Product Tester" review made the same point. One B&H owner reported that a leg-lock latch "snapped. It's cheap casted metal." That is one report, but it echoes others on the same page. If you shoot panoramas, budget for a pan clamp. For a head-to-head with the Manfrotto Befree line, which we did not review here, see our [Peak Design Travel Tripod vs Manfrotto Befree Advanced comparison](/blog/peak-design-travel-tripod-vs-manfrotto-befree-advanced/).

### Gitzo Traveler Series 1 GT1545T: lightest full-size legs

Legs only is the first thing to know about this listing: you get about 2.3 lb (1.06 kg) of carbon legs and a short column, rated to 22 lb (10 kg), and no head. [Gitzo's product page](https://www.gitzo.com/us-en/tripod-traveler-series-1-4-sections-gt1545tus/) lists 60.24 in maximum height, 8.66 in minimum and a 16.73 in fold; the 51.2 in column-down height comes from retailer listings. Four sections use G-Lock twist locks, the legs fold back over the column, and threaded feet accept spikes.

Backpackers pick it to keep the whole kit near 1.1 kg with a small head. On a DPReview thread, one owner wrote that after five-plus years and two Japan birding trips it "Works well with EOS R5 and 100-500L lens". The maker itself recommends lenses up to 135-200mm, so treat that 100-500mm use as one owner's experience, not the maker's rating.

The trade-offs are real. There are only two leg angles (25 and 70 degrees), so it can't get as low as a 90-degree design. Another DPReview owner warned that a small plastic key that stops the column turning "can fall out too easily"; the owner adds that it can take a while to replace. A third poster called it adequate at full height only in calm air or with smaller lenses.

### JOBY GorillaPod 5K Kit: wrap it around rocks and branches

Instead of standing on three straight legs, this flexible kit grips. Its flexible aluminum legs wrap around branches, rails and boulders, and the kit weighs 1.6 lb (739 g) with an 11 lb (5 kg) rating and an Arca-type ball head. It folds to about 15 in. JOBY does not publish a working height, so think of it as a support for a rest stop, not eye level.

The praise is specific. One B&H owner bought it to replace a JOBY lost on a hike, and another says it holds a Canon R5 with a battery grip.

Durability is the catch, and it's not one isolated report. Three B&H reviewers say a leg popped or broke off and could not be reattached, one of them on the third use. One wrote: "On assignment in remote Kenya, a leg popped off this brand new product and couldn't be put back on. This was only the third use." Another found the rubber rings on the leg spheres going sticky after about seven years, with no spare parts. Check every joint at home before the trip, and don't bend legs past their stops.

### Manfrotto PIXI EVO 2-Section Mini Tripod (MTPIXIEVO-BK): the 0.57 lb tabletop option

At 9.4 oz (0.26 kg) and 8.07 in folded, the PIXI EVO is the one tripod here you will never leave behind. [Manfrotto's spec page](https://www.manfrotto.com/global-en/pixi-evo-2-section-mini-tripod-black-light-and-compact-mtpixievo-bk/) gives a 2.5 kg (5.5 lb) safety payload; B&H lists 7.7 in maximum height. Two aluminum leg sections, two leg angles and an integrated ball head keep it simple.

Heavier kits push it too far. One B&H owner found the ball head post flexing with a Nikon Z6II, grip, 105mm macro and ring light, and another couldn't use it for vertical video with a 290 g Sony ZV-E10. A third says you need a wireless release, because touching the camera shakes the frame.

## Mini tripod for hiking or a full-size tripod?

**A mini tripod for hiking is enough only if you shoot from rocks, rails and ledges and never need standing height; for sunrise and sunset shots over a foreground, a full-size tripod earns its weight.** The PIXI EVO tops out at 7.7 in and the GorillaPod 5K has no published height, so both need something to stand on.

A full-size carbon fiber tripod pays for itself the moment you want a waterfall long exposure with an ND filter, a level horizon above trailside brush, or a 70-200mm at dusk. If your hikes are mostly about landscapes, our [landscape photography tripod picks for low angles and wind](/blog/landscape-photography-tripod/) go further on low-angle reach. Carrying a mini as well as a full-size tripod is a reasonable compromise for long trips, because 9.4 oz rarely changes a decision.

What about a trekking pole? Some hikers press one into service as a monopod, but a single pole is not a tripod, and for multi-second exposures we would not count on it.

## Wind, leg angles and uneven ground

**In wind, a backpacking tripod is steadiest with the center column down, the legs short and spread wide, and weight hanging from the ballast hook.** Every pick here loses stiffness as you go higher, so trade height for stability on exposed ground.

A raised column is a single thin post, so it amplifies every gust and every shutter press. Extend the thickest leg sections first and leave the thinnest ones for last. A wider leg angle lowers the camera and widens the base on slopes, but the Gitzo offers only two angles (25 and 70 degrees), so pick the wider one on exposed ground and accept a lower camera.

Use what you're already carrying for ballast. Hanging your pack from the ballast hook adds weight low in the frame, and a DPReview poster suggested "hanging a counter weight from the center column, ensuring not to exceed the weight limit." Keep the bag from swinging by letting it touch the ground lightly.

Feet matter on trail. Spiked feet grip soft ground, snow and ice; rubber feet are better on bare rock. The GT1545T's threaded feet accept spikes, and spikes are available for the Peak Design, though one owner notes they stop it fitting its case.

## Problems hikers run into, and the fix

**Five complaints come up again and again in hiking-tripod threads, and most have a fix in technique or in which tripod you pick.** We pulled them from DPReview forum threads and Reddit, and we've noted the matching pick for each.

### "Even the light tripods are too heavy"

Weight is a recurring complaint in DPReview hiking threads. Hikers already carrying about 7 lb of camera gear for waterfalls struggle to justify 2.8 lb more, and one DPReview poster said the carbon model, at around 2.8 lb, was the lightest they had found. Our fix: the GT1545T legs with a light head for the lowest full-size weight, or the PIXI EVO when any tripod is better than none.

### "Too small and wobbly for long exposures or a 100-400"

Photographers who add ND filters, astro or longer lenses outgrow small travel tripods, a recurring theme in DPReview forum threads. One poster wrote: ["i'm afraid the travel one is too small and wobbly for logn exposure with nd filters or astro or when using the 100-400"](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/threads/tripod-for-travel-hiking-long-exposure-stability-vs-portability.4575894/page-2). Our fix is load headroom and fewer joints: the Gitzo GT1545T's 22 lb rating and four-section legs or the Peak Design's 20 lb rating, with the column down and the pack on the hook. If your lens is longer than about 200mm, none of these four is built for it.

### Center column height vs stability

Reaching eye level often means raising the column, and owners know what that costs. One DPReview poster was blunt: ["The center column make the Travel Tripod less stable so you should not extend it."](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/threads/travel-tripod-stability-comparison.4834397/) Choose the most column-down height you can carry; the Peak Design (51.25 in) and the Gitzo (51.2 in) are level here. Our guide to [how tall a tripod should be for your height](/blog/how-tall-should-a-tripod-be-for-my-height/) shows the simple math, and a tilting screen makes chin height workable.

### Five leg sections and plastic locks

Experienced users warn that more sections and plastic flip locks mean more wobble and more parts to break, advice that turns up in DPReview threads. Leg sections and wobble go together: each joint adds a little play. If you shoot long exposures, the four-section GT1545T trades a slightly longer fold (16.7 in against 15.5 in) for fewer joints. With the five-section Peak Design, collapse the thinnest sections first.

### Integrated ball head with no friction control

Compact built-in heads are often on-off only. One DPReview hiker, writing about the Ulanzi head on a travel tripod, noted ["there is no friction control of the ball (it is pretty much on or off with a leaver)"](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/threads/lightest-tripod-for-hiking.4678070/). That makes fine framing with a heavy lens fiddly. The legs-only GT1545T lets you choose your own head with proper friction and pan controls, and the Peak Design owes its speed to a single ring, so budget for a pan clamp if you shoot panoramas.

## How to choose a hiking tripod

**Choose a hiking tripod in this order: weight with the head, column-down height, load against your real kit, then folded length, locks and plate.** The lightest number on a spec sheet means little if the tripod can't hold your lens at the height you shoot.

The checks we would run before buying:

- **Weight and material.** Carbon fiber is lighter for the same stiffness and damps vibration faster; aluminum costs less and survives knocks. Both full-size picks here are carbon fiber.
- **Folded length and carry.** 15 to 17 in fits most side pockets and pack straps; the PIXI EVO fits a coat pocket.
- **Max load capacity vs your camera.** A rated load is a ceiling, not a comfortable working load. Add your body, heaviest lens and head, and leave a wide margin for wind and a raised column.
- **Leg locks and sections.** A twist lock is slim and easy to grip with gloves but picks up grit; a flip lock shows at a glance that it's closed. Fewer sections are stiffer.
- **Packed length with the head.** Measure it against your pack before you buy; a travel tripod for hiking should strap to a backpack side without poking past the top.
- **Arca-Swiss compatibility.** An Arca-Swiss plate is the dovetail standard most heads and L-brackets share. The Peak Design and JOBY kits, which ship with a head, use Arca-type compatible plates; our [Arca-Swiss plate explainer](/blog/what-is-an-arca-swiss-plate-and-do-i-need-one/) covers the details.

If you mostly fly rather than hike, our roundup of [travel tripods for mirrorless cameras](/blog/best-travel-tripod-for-mirrorless-camera/) weighs carry-on fit instead of trail weight, and the [travel tripods hub](/category/travel-tripods/) lists every compact tripod we cover.

## Which hiking tripod should you buy?

**Buy the tripod that matches the heaviest lens you carry and the longest day you hike.** Here is how we would decide:

- **Mirrorless with a short zoom:** the carbon Peak Design Travel Tripod.
- **Longer telephoto or heavy kit:** none of these four is a heavy-lens tripod; the Gitzo is the sturdier of the two full-size picks on paper (22 lb against 20 lb), but its maker recommends lenses up to 135-200mm.
- **Multi-day backpacker who owns a head:** the Gitzo legs.
- **Minimalist or ultralight packer:** the PIXI EVO, or the GorillaPod if you shoot from trees and rails and check the joints first.

## Verdict on the best hiking tripod

**For most hikers, the best hiking tripod is the carbon Peak Design Travel Tripod; gram-counters should take the GT1545T legs, and minimalists a PIXI EVO or GorillaPod.** Each one solves a different part of that windy sunrise.

So for the hiker on the ridge with seven pounds of camera gear: if the kit is a mirrorless body and a 24-70mm, the carbon Travel Tripod adds 2.8 lb and stays near eye level with the column down. If it's a 70-200mm f/2.8, the Peak Design's 20 lb rating covers it, but keep the column down and the pack on the hook.
