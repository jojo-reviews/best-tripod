  - q: Is a carbon fiber tripod worth it for backpacking?
    a: >-
      Usually, if you hike often. Carbon fiber is lighter for the same stiffness
      and damps vibration faster; on the Peak Design Travel Tripod the carbon
      version saves about 0.6 lb over aluminum with the same height, fold and 20
      lb rating.
  - q: Can a mini tripod replace a full-size tripod on the trail?
    a: >-
      Only if you shoot from rocks, rails and ledges. The Manfrotto PIXI EVO
      tops out at 7.7 in and the JOBY GorillaPod 5K has no published working
      height, so neither gives standing height for a sunrise over a foreground.
  - q: How do I keep a lightweight tripod steady in wind?
    a: >-
      Keep the center column down, extend the thickest leg sections first,
      spread the legs wide and hang your pack from the ballast hook without
      exceeding the rated load. Fire with the self-timer or a remote so your
      hand adds no shake.
  - q: Is a 4-section or 5-section tripod better for hiking?
    a: >-
      A 4-section tripod such as the Befree GT or Gitzo GT1545T has fewer
      joints, so it is stiffer for long exposures. A 5-section design like the
      Peak Design folds shorter, to 15.5 in, which helps on a pack strap.

You reach the overlook twenty minutes before sunrise with seven pounds of camera gear already on your back, and the tripod strapped to the pack suddenly feels like the heaviest thing you own. Then the wind picks up on the ridge, you raise the center column to clear the rocks, and the long exposure comes back soft. The best hiking tripod is the one that solves both halves of that morning: light enough that you actually carry it, and stiff enough at the height you shoot. We built this guide around pack weight, wind and trail ground rather than airports and city breaks, and we matched six tripods to the hikers they suit. We did not use these tripods ourselves; every figure below comes from maker spec pages, retailer listings or owner reports, and we say which.

## Best hiking tripod picks by pack weight

**For most hikers, the best hiking tripod is a full-size carbon travel tripod around 2.3 to 2.8 lb that stands about 51 in with the center column down; a mini tripod only makes sense if you never need standing height.** Here is the quick answer by the kind of hiker you are.

These are our picks, from heaviest to lightest support:

## How we chose these hiking tripods

**We kept only tripods that at least two of the top-ranking hiking and backpacking guides recommend, then checked each one against maker pages, retailer spec tables and owner reviews.** We read Pack Hacker, We Seek Travel, Backwoods Pursuit, J.M. For each tripod we then checked the trail numbers that matter:

## Mini tripod for hiking or a full-size tripod?

**A mini tripod for hiking is enough only if you shoot from rocks, rails and ledges and never need standing height; for sunrise and sunset shots over a foreground, a full-size tripod earns its weight.** The PIXI EVO tops out at 7.7 in and the GorillaPod 5K has no published height, so both need something to stand on.

A full-size carbon fiber tripod pays for itself the moment you want a waterfall long exposure with an ND filter, a level horizon above trailside brush, or a 70-200mm at dusk. Our [best tripod for photography guide](/blog/best-tripod-for-photography/) matches full-size tripods to lens weight if you are still deciding how much tripod you need. If your hikes are mostly about landscapes, our [landscape photography tripod picks for low angles and wind](/blog/landscape-photography-tripod/) go further on low-angle reach. Carrying a mini as well as a full-size tripod is a reasonable compromise for long trips, because 9.4 oz rarely changes a decision.

What about a trekking pole? Some hikers press one into service as a monopod, but a single pole is not a tripod, and for multi-second exposures we would not count on it.

## Wind, leg angles and uneven ground

**In wind, a backpacking tripod is steadiest with the center column down, the legs short and spread wide, and weight hanging from the ballast hook.** Every pick here loses stiffness as you go higher, so trade height for stability on exposed ground.

A raised column is a single thin post, so it amplifies every gust and every shutter press. Extend the thickest leg sections first and leave the thinnest ones for last. A wider leg angle lowers the camera and widens the base on slopes, which is why the aluminum Travel Tripod's two notches frustrated one owner in wind, while the Befree GT's three angles give more in-between options.

Use what you're already carrying for ballast. Hanging your pack from the ballast hook adds weight low in the frame, and a DPReview poster suggested "hanging a counter weight from the center column, ensuring not to exceed the weight limit." Keep the bag from swinging by letting it touch the ground lightly.

Feet matter on trail. Spiked feet grip soft ground, snow and ice; rubber feet are better on bare rock. The GT1545T's threaded feet accept spikes, and spikes are available for the Peak Design, though one owner notes they stop it fitting its case.

## Problems hikers run into, and the fix

**Five complaints come up again and again in hiking-tripod threads, and most have a fix in technique or in which tripod you pick.** We pulled them from DPReview forum threads and Reddit, and we've noted the matching pick for each.

### "Even the light tripods are too heavy"

Weight is a recurring complaint in DPReview hiking threads. Hikers already carrying about 7 lb of camera gear for waterfalls struggle to justify 2.8 lb more, and one DPReview poster said the carbon model, at around 2.8 lb, was the lightest they had found. On an [r/Ultralight thread about the lightest full-frame tripod](https://www.reddit.com/r/Ultralight/comments/1dy4psz/most_ultralight_full_frame_tripod_not_taking_one/), skipping the tripod is a serious answer. Our fix: the GT1545T legs with a light head for the lowest full-size weight, or the PIXI EVO when any tripod is better than none.

### "Too small and wobbly for long exposures or a 100-400"

Photographers who add ND filters, astro or longer lenses outgrow small travel tripods, a recurring theme in DPReview forum threads. One poster wrote: ["i'm afraid the travel one is too small and wobbly for logn exposure with nd filters or astro or when using the 100-400"](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/threads/tripod-for-travel-hiking-long-exposure-stability-vs-portability.4575894/page-2). Our fix is load headroom: the Befree GT's 26.5 lb rating and four-section legs, with the column down and the pack on the hook.

### Center column height vs stability

Reaching eye level often means raising the column, and owners know what that costs. One DPReview poster was blunt: ["The center column make the Travel Tripod less stable so you should not extend it."](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/threads/travel-tripod-stability-comparison.4834397/) Choose the most column-down height you can carry; the Befree GT's 54.3 in leads here. Our guide to [how tall a tripod should be for your height](/blog/how-tall-should-a-tripod-be-for-my-height/) shows the simple math, and a tilting screen makes chin height workable.

### Five leg sections and plastic locks

Experienced users warn that more sections and plastic flip locks mean more wobble and more parts to break, advice that turns up in DPReview threads and a [read-before-buying thread on r/peakdesign](https://www.reddit.com/r/peakdesign/comments/1ee58bg/read_before_buying_a_peak_designs_tripod/). Leg sections and wobble go together: each joint adds a little play. If you shoot long exposures, the four-section Befree GT and GT1545T trade a slightly longer fold for fewer joints. With a five-section tripod, collapse the thinnest sections first.

### Integrated ball head with no friction control

Compact built-in heads are often on-off only. One DPReview hiker noted ["there is no friction control of the ball (it is pretty much on or off with a leaver)"](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/threads/lightest-tripod-for-hiking.4678070/). That makes fine framing with a heavy lens fiddly. The Befree GT's 496 head adds separate friction and pan controls, and the legs-only GT1545T lets you choose your own head.

## How to choose a hiking tripod

**Choose a hiking tripod in this order: weight with the head, column-down height, load against your real kit, then folded length, locks and plate.** The lightest number on a spec sheet means little if the tripod can't hold your lens at the height you shoot.

The checks we would run before buying:

- **Weight and material.** Carbon fiber is lighter for the same stiffness and damps vibration faster; aluminum costs less and survives knocks. On these two Peak Design versions, carbon saves 0.6 lb.
- **Folded length and carry.** 15 to 17 in fits most side pockets and pack straps; the PIXI EVO fits a coat pocket.
- **Max load capacity vs your camera.** A rated load is a ceiling, not a comfortable working load. Add your body, heaviest lens and head, and leave a wide margin for wind and a raised column.
- **Leg locks and sections.** A twist lock is slim and easy to grip with gloves but picks up grit; a flip lock shows at a glance that it's closed. Fewer sections are stiffer.
- **Packed length with the head.** Measure it against your pack before you buy; a travel tripod for hiking should strap to a backpack side without poking past the top.
- **Arca-Swiss compatibility.** An Arca-Swiss plate is the dovetail standard most heads and L-brackets share. All three full-size picks that ship with a head use Arca-type compatible plates; our [Arca-Swiss plate explainer](/blog/what-is-an-arca-swiss-plate-and-do-i-need-one/) covers the details.

If you mostly fly rather than hike, our roundup of [travel tripods for mirrorless cameras](/blog/best-travel-tripod-for-mirrorless-camera/) weighs carry-on fit instead of trail weight, and the [travel tripods hub](/category/travel-tripods/) lists every compact tripod we cover.
