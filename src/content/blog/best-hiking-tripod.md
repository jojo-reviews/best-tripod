  - q: How much should a hiking tripod weigh?
    a: >-
      Full-size travel tripods for hiking run about 2.3 to 3.4 lb in our picks,
      and you should compare them with a head attached. The Gitzo GT1545T is
      about 2.3 lb for legs only, the carbon Peak Design weighs 2.8 lb with its
      head built in, and the Befree GT Carbon kit weighs 3.4 lb with its ball
      head.
  - q: Is a carbon fiber tripod worth it for backpacking?
    a: >-
      Usually, if you hike often. Carbon fiber is lighter for the same stiffness
      and damps vibration faster; all three full-size picks here, the Peak
      Design Travel Tripod (Carbon Fiber), the Gitzo GT1545T and the Manfrotto
      Befree GT Carbon, are carbon fiber, from 2.3 lb legs only to 3.4 lb with
      a head.
  - q: Can a mini tripod replace a full-size tripod on the trail?
    a: >-
      Only if you shoot from rocks, rails and ledges. The Manfrotto PIXI EVO
      tops out at 7.7 in and the JOBY GorillaPod 5K has no published working
      height, so neither gives standing height for a sunrise over a foreground.
  - q: How do I keep a lightweight tripod steady in wind?
    a: >-
      Keep the center column down, extend the thickest leg sections first,
      spread the legs wide and hang your pack from the ballast hook without
      exceeding the rated load. Fire with the self-timer or a remote so your
      hand adds no shake.
  - q: What tripod should I take to Patagonia?
    a: >-
      Take a full-size carbon tripod you can run with the center column down,
      not a mini. Patagonia's wind is the deciding factor, so I'd pick the
      Manfrotto Befree GT Carbon (26.5 lb rating) or the Gitzo GT1545T legs,
      spread the legs wide and hang your pack from the hook.
  - q: Is a 4-section or 5-section tripod better for hiking?
    a: >-
      A 4-section tripod such as the Gitzo GT1545T has fewer joints, so it is
      stiffer for long exposures. A 5-section design like the Peak Design folds
      shorter, to 15.5 in, which helps on a pack strap.

You reach the overlook twenty minutes before sunrise with seven pounds of camera gear already on your back, and the tripod strapped to the pack suddenly feels like the heaviest thing you own. Then the wind picks up on the ridge, you raise the center column to clear the rocks, and the long exposure comes back soft. The best hiking tripod is the one that solves both halves of that morning: light enough that you actually carry it, and stiff enough at the height you shoot. I built this guide around pack weight, wind and trail ground rather than airports and city breaks, and I matched five tripods to the hikers they suit. We did not use these tripods ourselves; every figure below comes from maker spec pages, retailer listings or owner reports, and we say which.

## Best hiking tripod picks by pack weight

**For most hikers, the best hiking tripod is a full-size carbon travel tripod around 2.3 to 3.4 lb that stands 51 to 54 in with the center column down; a mini tripod only makes sense if you never need standing height.**

These are my picks, full-size tripods first, then the minis:

- **Best overall:** the [Peak Design Travel Tripod (Carbon Fiber)](amzn:Peak+Design+Travel+Tripod+Carbon+Fiber), 2.8 lb with its head built in, 15.5 in folded and rated to 20 lb.
- **Lightest full-size legs:** the [Gitzo Traveler GT1545T](amzn:Gitzo+GT1545T+Series+1+Carbon+4+Sections+Traveler+Tripod) at about 2.3 lb, legs only, so you add your own head.
- **Most load for wind:** the [Manfrotto Befree GT Carbon](amzn:Manfrotto+MKBFRTC4GT-BH+Befree+GT+Carbon+Travel+Tripod), 3.4 lb with its 496 ball head and rated to 26.5 lb.
- **Wrap-around mini:** the [JOBY GorillaPod 5K Kit](amzn:Joby+GorillaPod+5K+Kit) at 1.6 lb, for rocks, rails and branches.
- **Pocket backup:** the [Manfrotto PIXI EVO](amzn:Manfrotto+MTPIXIEVO-BK+PIXI+EVO+2-Section+Mini+Tripod) at 0.57 lb, a tabletop tripod for rest stops.

## How we chose these hiking tripods

**We kept only tripods that at least two of the top-ranking hiking and backpacking guides recommend, then checked each one against maker pages, retailer spec tables and owner reviews.** We read Pack Hacker, We Seek Travel, Backwoods Pursuit, J.M. Peltier and Matt Purciel; retailer list pages such as Best Buy's did not count as a recommendation. In this update I added the Befree GT Carbon, which We Seek Travel and Pixel Hiker both recommend for hiking. If you're still deciding on material, our [carbon fiber vs aluminum tripod for hiking](/blog/carbon-fiber-vs-aluminum-tripod-for-hiking/) guide covers the trade-off.

For each tripod we then checked the trail numbers that matter:

1. Weight with a head, not just the legs.
2. Folded length for a pack strap or side pocket.
3. Working height with the center column down.
4. Rated load against a real kit, such as a mirrorless camera with 70-200mm lens.
5. Leg sections, leg-lock type and how the head adjusts.
6. What owners praise and complain about after real hikes.

> **From our notes:** On the Gitzo GT1545T and carbon Peak Design spec sheets, the weight column turned out to be the trap. The Gitzo's 2.3 lb looks like the lightest full-size option, but that figure is legs and a short column only, while the carbon Peak Design's 2.8 lb already includes its ball head. Add even a small head to the Traveler and the gap nearly closes. Those two, by contrast, have almost the same column-down height, 51.2 and 51.25 in. That's why we compare weight with a head and height without the column, not the headline numbers.

## Mini tripod for hiking or a full-size tripod?

**A mini tripod for hiking is enough only if you shoot from rocks, rails and ledges and never need standing height; for sunrise and sunset shots over a foreground, a full-size tripod earns its weight.** The PIXI EVO tops out at 7.7 in and the GorillaPod 5K has no published height, so both need something to stand on, like every [best GoPro tripod](/blog/best-tripod-for-gopro/) pick.

A full-size carbon fiber tripod pays for itself the moment you want a waterfall long exposure with an ND filter, a level horizon above trailside brush, or a 70-200mm at dusk. If your hikes are mostly about landscapes, our [landscape photography tripod picks for low angles and wind](/blog/landscape-photography-tripod/) go further on low-angle reach. Carrying a mini as well as a full-size tripod is a reasonable compromise for long trips, because 9.4 oz rarely changes a decision.

What about a trekking pole? Some hikers press one into service as a monopod, but a single pole is not a tripod, and for multi-second exposures we would not count on it.

## Tripod for a Patagonia trek and other windy hikes

**For Patagonia, take a full-size carbon tripod you can run with the center column down and your pack on the hook; a mini won't hold up in that wind.** Landscape photographer Matt Purciel, writing about "the notorious winds of Patagonia," says he brought a Gitzo Series 1 Traveler kit (the same legs as my Gitzo pick, sold with a head) and a Benro Superslim, and reports both passed. In gusts he spreads the legs to their widest angle, skips the center column and hangs his camera bag from the hook.

Of my picks, I'd take the Befree GT Carbon for Patagonia-style wind, for its 26.5 lb rating and three leg angles, or the Gitzo legs if every ounce counts on a multi-day trek. Purciel rates the Peak Design Travel Tripod (aluminum) as slightly less stable than larger Gitzo models in high wind, so with it, I'd shorten the legs on exposed ground.

## Wind, leg angles and uneven ground

**In wind, a backpacking tripod is steadiest with the center column down, the legs short and spread wide, and weight hanging from the ballast hook.** Every pick here loses stiffness as you go higher, so trade height for stability on exposed ground.

A raised column is a single thin post, so it amplifies every gust and every shutter press. Extend the thickest leg sections first and leave the thinnest ones for last. A wider leg angle lowers the camera and widens the base on slopes, but the Gitzo offers only two angles (25 and 70 degrees), so pick the wider one on exposed ground and accept a lower camera.

Use what you're already carrying for ballast. Hanging your pack from the ballast hook adds weight low in the frame, and a DPReview poster suggested "hanging a counter weight from the center column, ensuring not to exceed the weight limit." Keep the bag from swinging by letting it touch the ground lightly.

Feet matter on trail. Spiked feet grip soft ground, snow and ice; rubber feet are better on bare rock, and our [tripod feet picks by surface](/blog/best-tripod-feet-for-sand-snow-and-rock/) add claws and snow shoes. The GT1545T's threaded feet accept spikes, and spikes are available for the Peak Design, though one owner notes they stop it fitting its case.

## Hiking tripod problems and the fix for each

**Five complaints come up again and again in hiking-tripod threads, and most have a fix in technique or in which tripod you pick.** We pulled them from DPReview forum threads and Reddit, and we've noted the matching pick for each.

### "Even the light tripods are too heavy"

Weight is a recurring complaint in DPReview hiking threads. Hikers already carrying about 7 lb of camera gear for waterfalls struggle to justify 2.8 lb more, and one DPReview poster said the carbon model, at around 2.8 lb, was the lightest they had found. My fix: the GT1545T legs with a light head for the lowest full-size weight, or the PIXI EVO when any tripod is better than none.

### "Too small and wobbly for long exposures or a 100-400"

Photographers who add ND filters, astro or longer lenses outgrow small travel tripods, a recurring theme in DPReview forum threads (for night work, see our [tripods for astrophotography](/blog/best-tripod-for-astrophotography/)). One poster wrote: ["i'm afraid the travel one is too small and wobbly for logn exposure with nd filters or astro or when using the 100-400"](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/threads/tripod-for-travel-hiking-long-exposure-stability-vs-portability.4575894/page-2). My fix is load headroom and fewer joints: the Befree GT Carbon's 26.5 lb rating or the Gitzo GT1545T's 22 lb rating, both on four-section legs, or the Peak Design's 20 lb rating, with the column down and the pack on the hook. If your lens is longer than about 200mm, none of these five is a dedicated heavy-lens tripod.

### Center column height vs stability

Reaching eye level often means raising the column, and owners know what that costs. One DPReview poster was blunt: "The center column make the Travel Tripod less stable so you should not extend it." Choose the most column-down height you can carry; the Befree GT Carbon leads at 54.3 in, while the Peak Design (51.25 in) and the Gitzo (51.2 in) are level. Our guide to [how tall a tripod should be for your height](/blog/how-tall-should-a-tripod-be-for-my-height/) shows the simple math, and a tilting screen makes chin height workable.

### Five leg sections and plastic locks

Experienced users warn that more sections and plastic flip locks mean more wobble and more parts to break, advice that turns up in DPReview threads. Leg sections and wobble go together: each joint adds a little play. If you shoot long exposures, the four-section GT1545T trades a slightly longer fold (16.7 in against 15.5 in) for fewer joints. With the five-section Peak Design, collapse the thinnest sections first.

### Integrated ball head with no friction control

Compact built-in heads are often on-off only. One DPReview hiker, writing about the Ulanzi head on a travel tripod, noted "there is no friction control of the ball (it is pretty much on or off with a leaver)". That makes fine framing with a heavy lens fiddly. The Befree GT Carbon's 496 head has its own friction and pan knobs, the legs-only GT1545T lets you choose your own head, and the Peak Design owes its speed to a single ring, so budget for a pan clamp if you shoot panoramas.

## How to choose a hiking tripod

**Choose a hiking tripod in this order: weight with the head, column-down height, load against your real kit, then folded length, locks and plate.**

The checks we would run before buying:

- **Weight and material.** Carbon fiber is lighter for the same stiffness and damps vibration faster; aluminum costs less and survives knocks. All three full-size picks here are carbon fiber.
- **Folded length and carry.** 15 to 17 in fits most side pockets and pack straps; the PIXI EVO fits a coat pocket.
- **Max load capacity vs your camera.** A rated load is a ceiling, not a comfortable working load. Add your body, heaviest lens and head, and leave a wide margin for wind and a raised column.
- **Leg locks and sections.** A twist lock is slim and easy to grip with gloves but picks up grit; a flip lock shows at a glance that it's closed. Fewer sections are stiffer.
- **Packed length with the head.** Measure it against your pack before you buy; a travel tripod for hiking should strap to a backpack side without poking past the top.
- **Arca-Swiss compatibility.** An Arca-Swiss plate is the dovetail standard most heads and L-brackets share. The Peak Design, Befree GT and JOBY kits, which ship with a head, use Arca-type compatible plates; our [Arca-Swiss plate explainer](/blog/what-is-an-arca-swiss-plate-and-do-i-need-one/) covers the details.

If you mostly fly rather than hike, our roundup of [travel tripods for mirrorless cameras](/blog/best-travel-tripod-for-mirrorless-camera/) weighs carry-on fit instead of trail weight, and the [travel tripods hub](/category/travel-tripods/) lists every compact tripod we cover.

- **Multi-day backpacker who owns a head:** the Gitzo legs.

## Verdict on the best hiking tripod

**For most hikers, the best hiking tripod is the carbon Peak Design Travel Tripod; windy-trail shooters should take the Befree GT Carbon, gram-counters the GT1545T legs, and minimalists a PIXI EVO or GorillaPod.**

So for the hiker on the ridge with seven pounds of camera gear: if the kit is a mirrorless body and a 24-70mm, the carbon Travel Tripod adds 2.8 lb and stays near eye level with the column down.
