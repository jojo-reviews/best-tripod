faqs:
  - q: Does a heavier tripod help long exposures?
    a: >-
      Yes, a heavier tripod helps long exposures because wind and shutter
      vibration move more mass less. Weight only helps if the legs are stiff and
      the column stays down, though. If you cannot carry a heavy tripod, hang
      your bag from the hook so it rests on the ground.
  - q: Carbon fiber or aluminum tripod for long exposure?
    a: >-
      Carbon fiber is the better tripod material for long exposure if you walk
      far, because it is lighter for the same stiffness and owners report it
      damps vibration better. Aluminum is cheaper and its extra weight helps in
      wind, which is why my overall pick, the Vanguard VEO 3+ 263AB, is
      aluminum.
  - q: Should the center column be up or down for long exposures?
    a: >-
      Down. The center column is the least stable part of a tripod, and owners
      report that a raised column lets even light cameras shake in wind. Extend
      the thickest leg sections first and raise the column only for a few
      centimeters if you must.
  - q: How much load capacity do I need for long exposure photography?
    a: >-
      A load capacity of about three times your camera and lens weight is plenty
      for long exposure photography. A 1.21 kg mirrorless kit needs about 3.6
      kg, and every tripod here is rated for 9.1 kg or more. Past that, mass and
      column-down height matter more than the rating.
  - q: Ball head or geared head for long exposure?
    a: >-
      A ball head is fine for most long exposures if it has friction control and
      is rated well above your kit. A geared head makes tiny framing moves
      easier for architecture and stacked ND filters, but it adds weight. Two
      picks here, the Benro TMA37C and Manfrotto 055, come without a head, so
      you can choose either.

The best tripod for long exposure is the one that stays still for 30 seconds or more in wind with its center column down. Load rating rarely decides it; mass, thick tripod legs and real column-down height do. My best overall pick is the [Vanguard VEO 3+ 263AB Aluminum Tripod with VEO BH-160S Ball Head](amzn:Vanguard+VEO3+263AB160S+aluminum+tripod+ball+head): 2.5 kg, a 15 kg rating and 148.3 cm of height without raising the column. If your long exposures are mostly night skies, our [tripods for astrophotography](/blog/best-tripod-for-astrophotography/) cover star trackers.

Picture a seascape at dusk with a 10-stop ND filter, a 60-second shutter and a breeze off the water. The waves turn to mist, but the rocks come out soft. The tripod moved, not the sea.

## Long Exposure Tripod Comparison by Mass, Height and Load

**A tripod for long exposure needs far less load capacity than its label suggests, so I compare mass and column-down height first.** A Nikon Zf weighs [about 710 g](https://onlinemanual.nikonimglib.com/zf/en/specifications_350.html) with battery and card, and the NIKKOR Z 24-70mm f/4 S adds 500 g. That 1.21 kg mirrorless camera kit uses a seventh to a sixteenth of each rating below; a full-frame body with a telephoto lens still fits easily.

This table compares my five long exposure tripods on the specs that decide a sharp 30-second frame.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vanguard VEO 3+ 263AB | 2.5 kg | 15 kg | 148.3 cm | 173.7 cm | 77 cm | 3 |
| Benro Mach3 TMA37C | 1.9 kg | 16 kg | 135 cm | 161.5 cm | 62.5 cm | 3 |
| Manfrotto 055 carbon | 2.1 kg | 20 kg | 140 cm | 170 cm | 54 cm | 4 |

| Peak Design Travel (Carbon) | 1.29 kg | 9.1 kg | 130.2 cm | 152.4 cm | 39.4 cm | 5 |

Here is my own math against that 1.21 kg kit:

- **Load headroom** runs from 7.5x (Peak Design) to 16.5x (Manfrotto 055), so every pick clears it.
- **Tripod mass against kit mass** is where they split: the VEO weighs about 2.1x the kit, the 055 and Befree about 1.7x, the Benro 1.6x and the Peak Design barely 1.07x.
- **Column-down height** spans 18 cm, from 130.2 to 148.3 cm, and that is the height you should actually use.

Mass matters because wind causes the vibration. A Beaufort force 4 moderate breeze of [13-18 mph](https://www.weather.gov/mfl/beaufort) already moves small branches, and at a 60-second shutter speed it keeps pushing on the legs.

But it showed us why two of these five come as legs only: the head is often the weaker link, which matches a DPReview poster whose legs held while the head wobbled.

## How to Set Up a Tripod for a Long Exposure

**A long exposure tripod stays sharp when you extend thick sections first, keep the column down, ground any weight and fire hands-free.** These steps work on all five:

1. Extend the thickest leg sections first and leave the thinnest ones collapsed.
2. Keep the center column fully down; raise it only a few centimeters to fine-tune.
3. Spread the legs wide and push the feet into firm ground, using spikes on sand or grass.
4. Hang your bag from the weight hook so it just touches the ground and cannot swing.
5. Turn off image stabilization; a forum poster on RF Shooters reports the IS "of some cameras will slowly drift during long exposures."
6. Fire with a remote release or a 2-second self-timer, and keep your hands off the tripod during the exposure.

## Long Exposure Tripod Problems Owners Run Into, and the Fix

**Most soft long exposures trace back to five tripod problems: wind, a raised column, a swinging bag, sinking feet and stabilization left on.**

**Wind through the legs.** On Photo Stack Exchange, one answer says "Wind will vibrate across the legs and transmit up to the camera." Stand upwind as a windbreak, lower the tripod and pick more mass, like the VEO. Our guide on [how to stop tripod shake in wind](/blog/how-to-stop-tripod-shake-in-wind/) has more fixes.

**A raised center column.** In a DPReview travel tripod comparison, one reply says "The center column make the Travel Tripod less stable so you should not extend it." Column-down height is the number to compare, which is why my table lists it.

**A swinging bag.** A reply in the DPReview windy-exposure thread says added weight "needs to be well thought out and grounded, not swinging, etc." Let the bag rest on the ground.

**Feet sinking in sand.** Lincoln Harrison found his spiked tripod "sinks further down as the water goes back out", so he reset after each wave. Push the feet in deep before you start, and see our [tripod feet for sand and rock](/blog/best-tripod-feet-for-sand-snow-and-rock/).

**Stabilization left on.** An RF Shooters owner wrote: "Turning off IS solved the problem."
