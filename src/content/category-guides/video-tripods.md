## How to choose a video tripod

**A video tripod should carry your full rig with margin, give you smooth, damped pans and tilts, and level quickly on uneven ground.** The head matters as much as the legs: a photo head that locks well for stills can still produce jerky moves on video.

### What to look for

- **A true fluid head.** A fluid head uses internal damping to add even resistance to pan and tilt, which turns a shaky hand movement into a steady camera move. Ball heads and photo pan-tilt heads lack that drag.
- **Payload against your whole rig.** Weigh the camera, lens, cage, monitor and microphone together, not just the body. Use the lowest published rating if listings disagree.
- **Drag and counterbalance.** Adjustable drag lets you slow a move; counterbalance is a spring that stops a heavy camera tipping forward when you tilt. Entry-level heads often have fixed drag and no counterbalance steps.
- **Leveling.** A bowl base (commonly 75mm or 100mm) lets you level the head without adjusting each leg. Bowl size also decides which heads you can upgrade to later.
- **Plate system.** Video plates vary widely, so check which plate the head uses and whether spares are easy to find.

### Key specs and trade-offs

- **Twin-tube vs single-tube legs.** Twin tubes with a mid-level spreader generally resist twisting during pans; single tubes save weight.
- **Height vs folded length.** Taller video tripods fold long and rarely fit a carry-on; plan on a tripod bag or checked luggage.
- **Weight.** A heavier tripod damps vibration but is harder to carry on foot.

### Who needs what

A beginner filming interviews or talking-head video from one spot is best served by a stable twin-leg kit with headroom for a cage and monitor. A shooter who moves on foot with a small mirrorless rig may prefer a lighter single-tube kit and accept a more basic head. Our guide to the [best video tripods with a fluid head for beginners](/blog/best-video-tripod-with-fluid-head-for-beginners/) compares three popular starter kits on payload, height, plates and head behavior, and explains how to set drag for a smooth pan.

For stills-first shooters who record the odd clip, a ball head on a panning base can handle locked-off shots, but regular video calls for a fluid head.

### Mistakes to avoid

- **Ignoring accessories in the payload.** A cage, monitor and microphone can add a few pounds.
- **Raising the center column for video.** It adds shake; extend the legs instead.
- **Panning with loose tilt.** Lock tilt when a shot ends to avoid spring-back.
- **Skipping leveling.** A pan on an unlevel head drifts the horizon across the frame.
- **Moving from the wrist.** Hold the pan handle near its end and move with your body.
