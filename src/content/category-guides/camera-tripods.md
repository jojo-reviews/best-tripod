## How to choose a full-size camera tripod

**A full-size camera tripod should put your viewfinder near eye level with the center column down, carry your heaviest lens with a wide margin, and use a head and plate system you can grow with.** Unlike a travel tripod, it is allowed to be long and heavy, so the questions shift from packed size to height, stiffness and control.

### What to look for

- **Working height without the center column.** This is the number that decides whether you stoop. A raised column adds height but turns three spread legs into a single thin post, which adds shake. As a rule of thumb, look for legs-only height of about your height minus 10 to 12 inches; our guide on [how tall a tripod should be for your height](/blog/how-tall-should-a-tripod-be-for-my-height/) explains the math and includes a chart.
- **Load capacity against real gear.** Look up your camera and lens makers' published weights, add the head, plate and any L-bracket, and keep the total well under the tripod's rating.
- **Minimum height.** Landscape and macro shooters often want a low leg-angle stop or a horizontal or reversible column.
- **Legs only or kit.** Many sturdy tripods are sold as legs only, so budget for a head and add its height to your calculation.

### Key specs and trade-offs

- **Three vs four leg sections.** Fewer sections are stiffer and quicker to set up; more sections fold shorter.
- **Flip vs twist locks.** Flip locks show at a glance whether they are closed; twist locks are slimmer but can pick up grit.
- **Aluminum vs carbon fiber.** Aluminum is the cheaper way to get height; carbon keeps tall legs lighter and settles vibration faster.
- **Ball head vs pan-tilt or geared head.** A ball head is fast and compact; a pan-tilt or geared head keeps axes separate for precise horizons. Our [ball head vs pan-tilt head guide](/blog/ball-head-vs-pan-tilt-head-for-landscape-photography/) compares them for landscape work.

### Who needs what

Shooters around average height with a mirrorless body and a mid-size zoom can work comfortably with a tripod that reaches the mid-50-inch range on its legs. Photographers over six feet need more, and very few spec sheets publish the column-down figure, so read reviews for measured numbers. Our roundup of the [best tripods for tall photographers over 6 feet](/blog/best-tripod-for-tall-photographers-over-6-feet/) compares four tall models and marks every spec the makers leave out.

If you drive to locations, weight matters less than stiffness. If you hike, a slightly shorter, lighter tripod you actually carry beats a tall one left in the car.

### Mistakes to avoid

- **Trusting the headline height.** It usually includes the fully raised column and excludes the head.
- **Extending the thinnest sections first.** Open the thickest leg sections first and leave the thinnest folded when you can.
- **Treating the rated load as a target.** It is a ceiling, and a tall tripod at full extension has less margin than its rating suggests.
- **Mixing plate systems.** Choose an Arca-Swiss compatible head if your other gear already uses Arca plates.
